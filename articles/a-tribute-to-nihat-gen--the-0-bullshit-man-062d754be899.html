A Tribute to Nihat Genc: The 0-Bullshit Man

In the software world we live in, there’s a term we all know too well: “BS.” Bullshit. It’s the smoke-and-mirrors talk, the meaningless meetings, the slippery justifications, the culture of saying something without saying anything. If you’ve worked long enough in this field — or really, in any industry — you’ve met people who lean on BS when they feel cornered or when they’re trying to hide a lack of substance.

But not everyone does. Choosing not to is a matter of character.

Today, I want to talk about someone who had zero tolerance for bullshit. A man who embodied honesty and sincerity so deeply that it shaped everything he did — and cost him plenty. His name was Nihat Genc, a Turkish writer, thinker, and intellectual who passed away today.

Most of my international friends have never heard of him. That’s expected — he wasn’t part of the globalized pop-media circus. He was, in his own words, a literary man who was pushed out of literature and into political and societal critique because he couldn’t stomach injustice, cruelty, or lies. He stepped out of the page and into public life, bringing his literary spirit with him.

What I admired most about Nihat wasn’t just his thinking — it was his emotional integrity. He didn’t just believe in truth; he lived by it. When he talked about war, corruption, or the identity crises that plague many in Turkey today, he spoke with the precision of a writer but the heart of someone who truly cared. He was an architect of ideas and a kind of societal engineer for lost souls. Not in a patronizing way, but in the way a lighthouse is useful to ships that forgot there even was a shore.

He was never interested in cheap popularity. He didn’t pander. He didn’t sugarcoat. As a result, the shiny, spicy, marketable media ignored him. He was “too real” to sell. And yet, he was never truly alone. I was there, listening, reading, learning. I wasn’t the only one.

Honesty and sincerity make people uncomfortable. In personal relationships, in teams, in organizations — and absolutely in politics. Why? Because we all carry flaws we’re trying to hide. When someone shows up without a mask, we see our own masks more clearly, and that can be painful. That’s true on an interpersonal level, but also on a national one. Nihat Genc didn’t just speak truth in the personal sense — he held up a mirror to a whole society.

He paid the price for it. I can’t even imagine the kinds of pressures and loneliness he faced.

But to people like me — and others who crave sincerity in a sea of posturing — he was a rare gem. A once-in-a-generation element.

And today, after months of declining health, he passed away. What surprised me wasn’t his death — it was how many people remembered him fondly. Part of me expected silence. He was never the darling of the mainstream. And yet, the wave of gratitude and remembrance I saw today was touching. And maybe hopeful.

So thank you, Nihat Abi. Thank you for sharing your mind with us, wrapped in art and carried with courage. You’ve given me, once again, a reason to try and stay honest in a world that often rewards the opposite.
